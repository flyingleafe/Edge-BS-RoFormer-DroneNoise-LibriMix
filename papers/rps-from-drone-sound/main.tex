\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Abstract}
The acoustic signature of a multirotor unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) is dominated by rotor harmonics whose fundamentals are set by the instantaneous rotational speeds (RPS) of individual rotors. Recovering per-rotor RPS directly from audio captured by onboard microphones would enable passive acoustic monitoring of the drone state without requiring access to onboard telemetry, with potential applications in flight-mode and manoeuvre inference, payload and fault diagnostics, model-based source separation, and conditioning-signal generation for downstream speech or event-detection systems. We conduct a pilot study of audio-only RPS estimation in the practically relevant regime where the rotor signal is mixed with non-rotor sound. We propose a lightweight convolutional regressor that takes the log-magnitude short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of the mixture as input and outputs the four per-rotor speeds at every STFT frame. The network is trained by frame-wise regression against telemetry-aligned ground truth. Experiments on synthetic mixtures and free-flight recordings demonstrate that the proposed model can reliably recover per-rotor RPS across both low- and high-SNR conditions.
\\\\
% On synthetic mixtures of LibriSpeech utterances and real quadcopter recordings from the DREGON dataset spanning $-30$ to $0$~dB signal-to-noise ratio, the model attains a coefficient of determination $\rsq = 0.84$, a mean absolute error of $0.56~\revps$, and a mean squared error of $5.15~(\revps)^{2}$ on held-out mixtures. The model also generalises to out-of-distribution high-SNR free-flight recordings with only a modest increase in error.

\textbf{Keywords}: drone acoustics; rotor speed estimation; harmonic noise; multirotor UAV
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================

Owing to their ability to hover stably and manoeuvre freely in three-dimensional space, multirotor drones have become increasingly attractive platforms for audition-based applications such as search and rescue~\shortcite{nakadai2017development, saklani2025towards}, acoustic surveillance~\shortcite{martinez2020review, shi2020acoustic}, and environmental sensing~\shortcite{wang2023drone, wang2026insitu}. Drone ego-noise generated by rotating motors and propellers is a major barrier to drone audition, as it can lead to extremely low signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) at onboard microphones. To address this challenge, a range of multi-microphone~\shortcite{wang2018acoustic, wang2020blind, wang2020deep, wang2022deep, clayton2023embedded}, single-microphone~\shortcite{mukhutdinov2023deep, liu2025bandsplit}, and audio-visual sensing~\shortcite{sanchez2017multi, wang2018tracking, wang2019audio} algorithms have been proposed to reduce the drone ego-noise at onboard microphone recording.

On the other hand, drone ego-noise is dominated by tonal components generated by the rotors, which provide an acoustic signature of a multirotor unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)~\shortcite{bernardini2017drone}. Since each rotor’s blade-passage frequency is directly determined by its rotational speed, the instantaneous speeds of all rotors jointly shape the low-frequency harmonic structure of the radiated sound field. Recovering these rotational speeds from passive microphone recordings would enable a range of practical tasks without access to onboard telemetry, including flight-mode and manoeuvre inference, payload and fault diagnostics, model-based source separation, and the conditioning of speech or acoustic event detection systems operating in the same scene. However, despite extensive research on drone ego-noise reduction, its rotor-induced signature has rarely been exploited as a source of state information.

%Drone audition has attracted growing interest for search-and-rescue~\cite{tan2025droneaudioset,wang2026skyear}, acoustic surveillance~\cite{garcia2025multiclass}, and robotic sensing~\cite{singh2025review}, yet the rotor signature itself is rarely exploited as a source of state information. 


%Drone audition has attracted growing interest for search-and-rescue~\cite{tan2025droneaudioset,wang2026skyear}, acoustic surveillance~\cite{garcia2025multiclass}, and robotic sensing~\cite{singh2025review}, yet the rotor signature itself is rarely exploited as a source of state information.

% Another active line of research addresses speech enhancement under drone ego-noise, where the rotor signal is treated as interference to be suppressed~\shortcite{mukhutdinov2023deep,liu2025bandsplit} or as a conditioning signal to guide separation~\shortcite{gulli2025rps}. 

Most prior work on drone acoustic signatures has focused on detection and classification, e.g. deciding whether a drone is present and, optionally, identifying its type~\shortcite{casabianca2021acoustic, bernardini2017drone}. Other publications focus on measuring and reducing noise pollution introduced by drones~\shortcite{konig2024prediction}, or utilizing noise itself for acoustic self-localization~\shortcite{serussi2024active}. Another active line of research addresses speech enhancement under drone ego-noise, where the telemetry-sourced rotor speeds are treated as a conditioning signal to guide separation~\shortcite{gulli2025enhancing, yen2023rotor}. The internal state of the drone, and in particular its per-rotor rotational speed, has rarely been treated as a target variable in its own right; where state is inferred from sound, the focus has been on fault or manoeuvre classification~\shortcite{altinors2021sound} rather than on continuous regression of rotational speed. Since the noise produced by a single rotor exhibits a fundamental frequency equal to the blade passing frequency, which is simply the rotational frequency times the number of rotor's blades, well-known pitch tracking methods such as YIN~\shortcite{de1990yin} or CREPE~\shortcite{kim2018crepe} might seem like natural approaches for inferring rotor speeds from ego-noise. However, these methods assume a single well-defined fundamental and do not support simultaneous tracking of four (or more) distinct variable pitches from equally prominent sources; moreover, they are not robust to competing broadband noise sources masking the lowest harmonics. On a quadcopter with four independent rotors, the problem reduces to single-pitch tracking only in the case where all four rotors are synchronised, which is almost never true in-flight. The version of the problem we consider in this paper is deliberately the one that matters for practical downstream use: estimating per-rotor speed from a mixture that contains a non-rotor signal in addition to the drone noise recorded in-flight. For the aforementioned reasons, the application of standard pitch-tracking techniques cannot yield useful results in this setup. While propeller speeds have been successfully estimated with advanced visual sensors (event cameras)~\shortcite{chen2026count}, audio-only estimation from a mixture has not yet been studied. 

Our contribution is a first study of audio-only multi-rotor RPS estimation in this practically relevant scenario. We frame the task as a frame-wise regression problem over the short-time Fourier transform of the mixture, and show that a lightweight convolutional network recovers per-rotor speed across an SNR range of $-30$ to $0$~dB on synthetic mixtures,
with strong agreement to ground-truth telemetry. We further verify that the model generalises to real high-SNR free-flight recordings from the DREGON dataset~\shortcite{strauss2018dregon}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Problem formulation}

Let $x[n]$ be a single-channel mixture sampled at \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} consisting of a
speech signal and the acoustic signature of a four-rotor UAV.
Let $\mathbf{X}(t, f)$ denote its short-time Fourier transform with window size
$N = 2048$ and hop $H = 512$, giving frequency resolution $\approx \SI{7.8}{\hertz}$ and
frame rate $\approx \SI{31.25}{\hertz}$.
The task is to predict, at every STFT frame $t$, the four rotational speeds
$\mathbf{r}(t) = (r_{1}(t), \dots, r_{4}(t)) \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ in revolutions
per second.

For a rotor with $b$ blades rotating at $f_{r}$ revolutions per second, the
blade-passage frequency is $b f_{r}$ and the radiated pressure field contains
pronounced harmonics at $k b f_{r}$, $k = 1, 2, \dots$.
The four time-varying rotational speeds therefore determine the dominant
low-frequency structure of the acoustic signature.

We train a fully convolutional network $f_{\theta}: \mathbb{R}^{F \times T} \to \mathbb{R}^{4 \times T}$
that maps the log-magnitude spectrogram $\log(1 + |\mathbf{X}|)$ to per-frame per-rotor
speed predictions. The network is trained by minimising the mean squared error
between predictions and telemetry-aligned ground truth,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)
    = \frac{1}{4T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{4}
      \bigl(\hat{r}_{i}(t) - r_{i}(t)\bigr)^{2},
\end{equation}
which serves as the training cost function.
Here $F = 1025$ is the number of frequency bins and $T = L/H + 1$ is the
resulting number of STFT frames for an input clip of $L$ samples.

\subsection{Dataset: DREGON-LM}
\label{sec:dataset}

We build a synthetic mixture dataset, DREGON-LM, by mixing
LibriSpeech~\shortcite{panayotov2015librispeech} \emph{train-clean-100} utterances
with the in-flight noise recordings of DREGON~\shortcite{strauss2018dregon}, which
provides synchronised four-rotor tachometer telemetry.
Each clip is $L = 131{,}584$ samples ($\approx 8.22$~s) at \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} -- we downsample clips from DREGON from the original sample rate of \SI{48}{\kilo\hertz}.
For every clip we sample a target SNR uniformly in $[-30, 0]$~dB, scale the
noise accordingly, and rescale the mixture to avoid clipping.
We generate $6000$ training and $600$ validation mixtures.

Ground truth is provided by per-rotor tachometer streams in the DREGON dataset.
The native motor-tachometer sampling rate is approximately \SI{950}{\hertz},
whereas the model operates at the STFT frame rate
$\approx \SI{31.25}{\hertz}$ ($H = 512$ at \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}).
We therefore linearly interpolate the raw tachometer traces onto the STFT time
grid (one target sample per STFT frame) using PyTorch's
\texttt{F.interpolate} with \texttt{mode="linear"} and
\texttt{align\_corners=False}.
This means the model is trained to reproduce the rotor-speed trajectory at the
STFT frame rate; it is not asked to resolve sub-frame dynamics.
Both prediction and target therefore share exactly the same temporal grid,
making the comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} and the scalar metrics
reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} directly comparable.

The train/validation split is performed at the clip level, not at the flight
level, so the two sets draw from the same underlying DREGON recordings; this
is a limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Lightweight convolutional regressor}
\label{sec:simpleconv}

Our model, \emph{SimpleConv}, is a small fully convolutional network
operating on the log-magnitude STFT $\log(1 + |\mathbf{X}|)$ of the mixture.
The encoder consists of five $2$-D convolutional blocks, each comprising
Conv2D~$\to$~BatchNorm~$\to$~LeakyReLU (negative slope $0.2$).
The blocks downsample along frequency while preserving the time axis intact.
Table~\ref{tab:arch} gives the exact layer specification.

    $131{,}584$ samples ($\approx 8.22$~s at \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}),
    giving $T = 258$ STFT frames.
  }
  \label{tab:arch}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{clccccc}
    \toprule
    Block & Layer & In ch. & Out ch. & Kernel & Stride & Output (freq, time) \\
    \midrule
    Input & log-magnitude STFT & 1 & 1 & --- & --- & $(1025, 258)$ \\
    Enc 1 & Conv2d + BN + LeakyReLU & 1 & 45 & $(7, 5)$ & $(2, 1)$ & $(513, 258)$ \\
    Enc 2 & Conv2d + BN + LeakyReLU & 45 & 90 & $(7, 5)$ & $(2, 1)$ & $(257, 258)$ \\
    Enc 3 & Conv2d + BN + LeakyReLU & 90 & 90 & $(5, 3)$ & $(2, 1)$ & $(129, 258)$ \\
    Enc 4 & Conv2d + BN + LeakyReLU & 90 & 90 & $(5, 3)$ & $(2, 1)$ & $(65, 258)$ \\
    Enc 5 & Conv2d + BN + LeakyReLU & 90 & 90 & $(5, 3)$ & $(2, 1)$ & $(33, 258)$ \\
    Pool & Spatial mean over freq & 90 & 90 & --- & --- & $(90, 258)$ \\
    Head 1 & Conv1d + ReLU + Dropout($0.1$) & 90 & 64 & $5$ & $1$ & $(64, 258)$ \\
    Head 2 & Conv1d & 64 & 4 & $1$ & $1$ & $(4, 258)$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

After the fifth encoder block the feature tensor has shape
$(B, 90, 33, T)$, where $B$ is batch size, $90$ is the channel count, $33$ is
the remaining frequency bins, and $T$ is the number of time frames.
We collapse the frequency axis by a spatial average (mean over the $33$ bins)
to obtain a sequence of $90$-dimensional frame embeddings of shape $(B, 90, T)$.
A two-layer $1$-D convolutional head then projects each frame embedding to the
four rotor-speed predictions.
The first head layer (Conv1D kernel $5$, padding $2$, $90 \to 64$ channels,
followed by ReLU and Dropout with rate $0.1$) mixes information across
neighbouring frames, while the second layer (Conv1D kernel $1$, $64 \to 4$
channels) is a linear projection per frame.
The total parameter count is just $538,164$ -- less than half a million, a very small model by modern standards. The model of this size is well-suited for CPU inference -- it processes 1 second of \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} audio in $\approx$\SI{15}{\milli\second} on Intel Core Ultra 7 165U \SI{4.9}{\giga\hertz} on average, yielding a real-time factor of $\approx 67.0\times$.
The full architecture is shown schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}.

  \caption{SimpleConv architecture. Each encoder block (yellow) halves the
           frequency dimension while preserving time; channel count grows

\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:training}

We train SimpleConv from scratch with AdamW~\shortcite{loshchilov2019adamw}
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$)~\shortcite{zhou2024towards}, batch size~$16$,
mixed-precision (\texttt{torch.cuda.amp}), and gradient clipping at~$5.0$.
The learning rate is halved when the validation MSE does not improve for five
consecutive epochs, and training stops when the validation MSE does not improve
for ten consecutive epochs; the model converges in under $100$ epochs.
All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and are fully deterministic when a
fixed random seed is provided.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We report three complementary regression metrics to evaluate the rotor speed estimation performance: mean squared error, mean absolute error and coefficient of determination. 

\textit{Mean squared error (MSE)} is computed
    per frame over all rotors and clips, in units of $(\revps)^{2}$:
    \begin{equation}
      \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{clips}}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\text{clips}}}
        \frac{1}{4T_{c}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}
        \bigl(\hat{r}_{i}^{(c)}(t) - r_{i}^{(c)}(t)\bigr)^{2}.
    \end{equation}
    MSE penalises large errors quadratically and is the quantity the network is
    trained to minimise. It is sensitive to outlier frames and gives a precise,
    differentiable training target, but its scale is less intuitive than an error
    in rev/s.

\textit{Mean absolute error (MAE)} is computed per clip by time-averaging the predictions and targets for each
    rotor, then taking the mean absolute difference across rotors and clips,
    in units of $\revps$:
    \begin{equation}
      \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{clips}}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\text{clips}}}
        \frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4} \frac{1}{T_{c}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{c}}
        \bigl|\hat{r}_{i}^{(c)}(t) - r_{i}^{(c)}(t)\bigr|.
    \end{equation}
    MAE is linear in the error and therefore more interpretable for
    practitioners: an MAE of $0.56~\revps$ means the predicted time-averaged
    rotor speed is on average half a revolution per second away from the truth.

\textit{Coefficient of determination ($\rsq$)} is computed per clip as
    \begin{equation}
      R^{2}_{c,i} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{c}}
        \bigl(\hat{r}_{i}^{(c)}(t) - r_{i}^{(c)}(t)\bigr)^{2}}
        {\sum_{t=1}^{T_{c}} \bigl(r_{i}^{(c)}(t) - \bar{r}_{i}^{(c)}\bigr)^{2}},
      \qquad
      R^{2} = \frac{1}{4N_{\text{clips}}}\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\text{clips}}}
        \sum_{i=1}^{4} R^{2}_{c,i},
    \end{equation}
    where $\bar{r}_{i}^{(c)}$ is the per-clip mean of the ground-truth targets
    for rotor $i$. The per-clip $\rsq$ values are then macro-averaged across the
    validation set. This metric asks: ``does the model track the \emph{temporal
    trajectory} of each rotor within a clip better than simply predicting the
    clip mean?'' A value of $\rsq = 1$ indicates perfect tracking; $\rsq = 0$
    means the model is no better than the per-clip mean predictor; negative
    values indicate performance worse than the mean. Because it is normalised
    by the per-clip variance, $\rsq$ is comparable across clips with very
    different absolute speed ranges.


\subsection{Results on held-out synthetic mixtures}
\label{sec:heldout}

We evaluate SimpleConv on the $600$ held-out DREGON-LM validation clips.
The model reaches $\rsq = 0.835$, MAE $= 0.56~\revps$, and
MSE $= 5.15~(\revps)^{2}$ at its best validation epoch (epoch~$73$).
A naive baseline that predicts the per-rotor training mean at every frame gives
MSE $\approx 365~(\revps)^{2}$; the trained model reduces the error by close to
two orders of magnitude.

Fig.~\ref{fig:training} shows the training dynamics.
Validation MSE drops by roughly two orders of magnitude in the first ten epochs
and then settles into a slow refinement phase; the best validation $\rsq$
($0.835$) is reached after epoch~$73$.
Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows qualitative predictions on three randomly
selected validation clips.
The four rotors operate at clearly distinct speeds, and the predictions track
each rotor individually rather than collapsing onto a single average value.
Most of the residual error consists of fast, low-amplitude fluctuations on top
of an otherwise correct trajectory.

To understand how performance varies across the SNR range, we partition the
$600$ validation clips into six equally spaced $5$~dB bins from $-30$ to $0$~dB
and compute MSE and $\rsq$ per bin.
Figure~\ref{fig:per-snr} shows the result.
For the RPS estimation task the drone harmonics are the signal and the speech
is the interfering noise; consequently higher-SNR mixtures contain stronger
speech that increasingly masks the rotor harmonics.
MSE stays between $4.2$ and $6.6~(\revps)^{2}$ and $\rsq$ between $0.77$ and
$0.88$ across the entire range, with the modest degradation above $-5$~dB
consistent with the expected masking effect.

\subsection{Results on real high-SNR free-flight recordings}
\label{sec:highsnr}

The synthetic recordings in the validation set use noise recordings chunks from the same noise-only DREGON flight recordings as used for forming the training set. Even though no individual noise chunk is shared between training and validation mixture sets, it still means that the model is validated on exactly the same flight conditions as seen in the training set; moreover, our method of additive synthetic mixing might not precisely replicate physical acoustic mixing during the recording, due to possible reverberation, fluctuating distance to the speech source mid-flight, etc. Therefore, to validate that method generalizes, we additionally evaluate on out-of-distribution clips taken directly
from the DREGON \emph{free-flight speech-high room1} recording, where a
speech source is played alongside the drone but the speech energy comes from a
separate speaker. We deliberately select a higher-SNR recording, which exhibits SNR in range [-3, -8] dB, in order to test the method's robustness in the presence of a prominent speech signal from a nearby source.
We run SimpleConv on the entire free-flight recording without re-training
($\approx 47$~s of audio with synchronised motor telemetry) and compute
per-frame MSE against the interpolated ground-truth RPS.
Input audio is peak-normalised to $0.9$, matching the normalisation used during
training.

Figure~\ref{fig:fullsequence} shows the result.
The top panel is the log-magnitude spectrogram of the full sequence; the
middle panel overlays ground-truth (dotted) and predicted (solid) rotor speeds
for all four rotors; the bottom panel plots the per-frame MSE (smoothed with a
$1$~s moving average).
Grey shaded bands mark the takeoff ($0$--$4.5$~s) and landing
($46.4$--$47$~s) phases, during which rotor speeds are below $50~\revps$.

For the bulk of the recording, where the drone is in steady flight, the
model tracks the four rotors accurately and the per-frame MSE stays within a
factor of two of the held-out synthetic-mixture MSE ($5.15~(\revps)^{2}$).
The mean MSE over all in-flight frames (rotor speeds $> 50~\revps$) is
$19.9~(\revps)^{2}$, a modest increase over the in-distribution value that
is consistent with the higher SNR and different acoustic conditions of the
real recording.

The largest errors occur during takeoff and landing, when the rotors spin up
from or down to zero.  The model has not seen these regimes in training:
DREGON-LM was constructed from sustained in-flight noise.  During landing,
for example, the ground truth collapses to zero for all rotors while the
acoustic scene still contains residual rotor-like sounds (motor wind-down,
contact noise).  The model therefore continues to predict in-flight speeds,
giving large frame-wise errors.  This failure mode is one of distribution
shift rather than of audio-only RPS estimation per se.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

Three limitations bound the conclusions of this study.
First, both training and evaluation use a single quadcopter platform (the DREGON
drone); generalisation across airframes, propellers and microphone configurations
is untested and is the natural next experiment. However, it would require additional independent noise recordings aligned with telemetry data, which is not readily available outside the DREGON dataset.
Second, the held-out synthetic set shares underlying drone recordings with the
training set (different clips, same flights), so part of $\rsq$ reflects
in-distribution interpolation; the high-SNR free-flight analysis is the strongest
evidence towards generalization between flights, but it remains small.
Third, ground-truth speeds are interpolated from a motor-sampled tachometer onto
the STFT time grid, so fast transients are smoothed and we do not consider high-frequency discrepancies with the ground truth measurement as errors; this might not be an issue for some downstream applications, but any potential future method aiming for extremely precise predictions (e.g. inferring instantaneous rotor phase) would not be able to succeed with the same output signal resolution.

The most immediate follow-up is a careful comparison against classical baseline pitch-tracking methods which could be adapted for multi-source usecase: cepstral analysis, harmonic-product spectra and/or matched-filter banks indexed by RPS. This would quantify exactly what the learned model buys over signal processing, especially in the presence of strong additional signals; however, that would also require some innovation which would make such methods applicable to estimation of multiple pitches simultaneously.
A second natural direction is exploring different predictor architectures and comparing their performance with the simple convolutional predictor presented here; it is quite likely that larger or slightly more complex models could easily improve upon the prediction quality of our simple model.
A third direction is to use the predicted RPS as a conditioning signal for
downstream tasks (speech enhancement, drone-state classification) and to measure
the tolerance of each task to RPS prediction error; this tolerance is currently
assumed rather than known.
Finally, training and evaluation on additional UAV platforms, and on real
low-SNR flight recordings rather than synthetic mixtures, would strengthen the
generalisation claim.

In conclusion, we have presented a first study of audio-only rotor speed estimation for multirotor UAVs in the presence of strong non-rotor sound.
A small convolutional regressor trained on synthetic LibriSpeech--DREGON mixtures
predicts per-rotor speed across an SNR range of $-30$ to $0$~dB with
$\rsq = 0.84$ and a mean absolute error of $0.56~\revps$, and remains accurate
on real high-SNR free-flight recordings except in flight regimes (landing) not
represented in the training distribution.
The result establishes that the dominant acoustic structure of a multirotor in
flight carries enough information about its rotational state to make passive
acoustic monitoring of drone state feasible without onboard telemetry, and
motivates a more systematic study of audio-only drone-state estimation as a
useful primitive in its own right.
